Custom Plan - Funding Options

If Your 529 Falls Short

Before you fine-tune your plan, here are the ways families usually cover a gap. In the custom planner you can add funding sources month by month, but only in months where your projected 529 balance goes negative, and never more than the shortfall.

1. Borrow from your 401(k)

You can borrow the lesser of 50% of your vested balance or $50,000, minus loans still outstanding. Education loans are generally repaid within 5 years, and the interest goes back into your own account. Risk: the borrowed money stops growing, and if you leave your job or miss payments the unpaid balance can be taxed, plus a 10% penalty under age 59½. Not every plan offers loans.

2. Federal student loans

Fixed rates (6.52% undergraduate and 8.07% graduate for loans first disbursed July 2026 to June 2027), a standard 10-year repayment, and protections such as income-driven repayment and deferment. Annual and total borrowing limits apply, and Direct Loans carry about a 1% fee.

3. Early retirement withdrawals

  • Roth IRA: contributions come out tax-free and penalty-free. Earnings taken early can be taxed.
  • Traditional / SEP IRA: exempt from the 10% penalty for qualified college costs, but still taxed as income.
  • 401(k) / 403(b): taxed as income plus a 10% penalty before age 59½ (no tuition exception), and plans usually allow withdrawals only for hardship.

Withdrawals are never repaid and permanently reduce your retirement savings.

4. Private student loans

Rates depend on credit and cosigner (about 14% is used as a national average here), and there are no federal protections. Usually the last resort.

The Simple plan applies these options year by year to cover only each year's gap, from lowest estimated cost to highest. All figures are estimates, ignore state tax, and are not financial or tax advice. Please consult a licensed professional.

About One-Time Lump Sums

A one-time lump sum is an extra, single contribution to the 529 account on top of your regular schedule. Examples:

  • Birth anniversary gift: contribute to your child's 529 account on each of their birthdays.
  • Bonus: plan to keep aside a portion of your annual bonus.
  • Tax refund: plan to direct a portion of your tax refund check into the 529.

Tip: After adding a lump sum, click the +1 Yr button on its row to repeat it for the next year. Keep clicking to repeat it each year until your child graduates.

IRS 529 Rules: Off-Campus & Living at Home Allowances

Under IRS Code Section 529(e)(3)(B), students enrolled at least half-time at an eligible educational institution (including Community Colleges) can use 529 distributions tax-free for room and board expenses even if they do not live in official campus housing.

1. Off-Campus Apartment Allowance ($8,000/yr base)

The tax-free 529 withdrawal amount for off-campus rent and food & groceries is capped at the official "Room and Board Cost of Attendance (COA)" standard set by the college's financial aid office for off-campus students.

2. Living with Parents Boarding Allowance ($4,000/yr base)

Even when living at home, the IRS permits students to withdraw tax-free 529 funds up to the institution's official COA room and board allowance for students living with parents (covering food, groceries, and housing costs). Parents can document reasonable housing and meal reimbursements provided to the student.

Qualified 529 Expenses

Under IRS guidelines, tax-free distributions from a 529 plan can be used to pay for Qualified Higher Education Expenses (QHEE) at eligible post-secondary institutions:

  • Tuition & Mandatory Fees: Full cost of enrollment and required campus student fees.
  • Room & Board: Campus dorms, meal plans, or off-campus housing/food up to the college's official COA limit.
  • Books & Required Supplies: Textbooks, lab supplies, software, and tools required for coursework.
  • Computers & Peripheral Equipment: Laptops, desktop hardware, internet access, and educational software used by the student.

Non-Qualified Personal Expenses

Expenses that are NOT eligible for tax-free 529 withdrawals. Using 529 funds for these items will trigger ordinary income tax plus a 10% IRS penalty on earnings:

  • Campus Travel & Transportation: Gas, parking passes, public transit, flights home, or vehicle upkeep.
  • Personal & Lifestyle Essentials: Clothing, laundry, phone bills, streaming subscriptions, and entertainment.
  • Health & Insurance: Health insurance policies, medical bills, and OTC medicines.
  • Application & Test Fees: SAT/ACT fees, college application fees, or graduation costs.
